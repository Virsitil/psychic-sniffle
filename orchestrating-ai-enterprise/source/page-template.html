What we believe the research is telling us

As AI moves into production, we're seeing two shifts happen at once.

Shift 01

AI is expanding what enterprises need to orchestrate.

AI-driven workflows and agents are becoming part of business processes that depend on data, applications, infrastructure, and other enterprise systems.

Shift 02

AI is changing how enterprises orchestrate.

AI is already helping teams design and troubleshoot workflows, with emerging capabilities pointing toward more intelligent and adaptive execution.

Together, these shifts expand the role of enterprise orchestration. AI is becoming part of business execution while also influencing how that execution happens.

As AI takes on more responsibility, enterprises may need to coordinate workflows that can adapt to changing conditions while delivering reliable business outcomes.

The AI Execution Paradox: a forward-looking perspective

The more the execution path can change, the more important orchestration becomes.

Today, teams typically define workflow steps in advance. As AI takes on more responsibility, organizations could increasingly define the desired outcome, dependencies, and boundaries, with orchestration adapting more of the execution path at runtime. The challenge shifts from managing a predefined sequence of steps to coordinating execution that can adapt as conditions change.

Less predetermined execution requires more deliberate orchestration.

As execution paths become less predefined, organizations need clearer boundaries around what AI agents can do, how their actions are monitored, and who is accountable for the results. Orchestration must also account for dependencies across workflows and support recovery when conditions change or execution fails.

Enterprise environments will remain diverse, and different teams may develop and deploy AI. Delivering the broader business outcome requires clear ownership of dependencies and accountability across workflows, and IT Ops can apply its experience managing business-critical workflows to help establish the operational controls needed for reliable delivery.

Executive summary

Speed is the yardstick.
Reliability is the risk.

As AI moves into production, speed is becoming a measure of success. But getting workflows up and running is just one part of the challenge. The real test is whether they deliver the outcomes the business depends on. Faster delivery can help organizations realize value from AI sooner, but a workflow also needs to execute reliably across the systems and processes it depends on. That’s where enterprise orchestration comes in.

02
Finding 02

AI becomes part of what enterprises need to orchestrate

Once AI enters production, it becomes part of a broader system of enterprise execution.

AI value increasingly depends on what happens outside the AI.

Models and agents may generate intelligence or initiate action, but the business outcome depends on data, applications, systems, workflows, and people working together.

Consider a payroll process that uses AI to identify anomalies. The AI might flag an issue correctly, but employees still depend on accurate data reaching the payroll system and payments being processed on time. The value comes from the entire workflow delivering the outcome, not just the AI performing its task.

Among respondents orchestrating AI, ML, or agent-driven workflows in production or pilot, respondents report using orchestration to:

Monitor AI workflow reliability and SLAs41%
Source: BMC FY27 Global Findings, Q49. Production/pilot respondents (n = 1,434).

This is the first major shift: AI expands what enterprises need to orchestrate.

The CIO conversation

“AI workflows increasingly depend on the systems and business processes we already run. We need to coordinate the dependencies between AI and our enterprise systems so the entire workflow delivers the intended business outcome.”

Finding 04

The two shifts converge

AI is becoming both part of enterprise execution and a force shaping how that execution happens.

AI-driven workflows and agents are becoming part of business processes. At the same time, AI-enabled orchestration can help monitor execution, detect changing conditions, optimize decisions, and support recovery.

These two shifts point to connecting new AI capability to dependable enterprise execution, without requiring every team to use the same tool.

Adaptive execution extends that perspective by asking how coordination, oversight, and recovery would need to evolve if more of the execution path could change at runtime.

Looking ahead

Where we think this is going

Finding 08

The opportunity for orchestration is expanding.

New AI-driven workflows, data pipelines, cloud services, and applications are becoming part of enterprise execution alongside the systems and processes already running the business.

As orchestration reaches across more of that landscape, enterprises need stronger coordination and visibility across the complete business outcome.

The opportunity is to extend orchestration to the new work shaping the enterprise while connecting it to the environments the business already depends on.

Conclusion

Turning AI capability into reliable business outcomes

What we believe this adds up to

The value of enterprise orchestration is rising.

  1. The scope of enterprise orchestration is expanding

    AI-driven workflows and agents, data pipelines, cloud services, and modern applications are becoming part of enterprise execution alongside the established systems and processes businesses already depend on.

  2. AI is also expanding the role of orchestration

    AI can increasingly help enterprises move from configuring every step of execution toward defining the outcome, dependencies, and boundaries, with orchestration adapting more of the path as conditions change.

  3. Together, those changes raise the value of enterprise orchestration

    As workflows span more technologies and teams, the buyer benefit is reliable delivery across the complete business outcome. IT operations, data, and AI leaders need to consider dependencies, visibility, ownership, governance, and recovery across their existing platforms. More adaptive execution could increase the importance of those disciplines.

That brings us back to the idea at the beginning:

AI can provide intelligence. Enterprise execution produces the outcome.

And to the opportunity we believe the research reveals:

The goal isn’t simply to orchestrate more workloads. It’s to help increasingly complex and adaptive workflows deliver the business outcomes they’re designed to support.

Questions and answers

Frequently asked questions

What is enterprise orchestration?

+

Enterprise orchestration coordinates workflows across AI, data, cloud, applications, infrastructure, and people so business processes are delivered reliably from end to end.

How are enterprises using AI within orchestration today?

+

Among production and pilot respondents, 50% report AI-assisted troubleshooting and 48% AI-assisted workflow design. Respondents also expect value from cost optimization (50%), anomaly detection (43%), and self-healing workflows (41%).

Why does AI increase the need for orchestration?

+

AI value depends on what happens outside the AI. Among respondents orchestrating AI, ML, or agent-driven workflows in production or pilot, 55% use their orchestration platforms to integrate AI workflows with business processes and 53% to manage dependencies between AI and enterprise systems.

What is the AI Execution Paradox?

+

BMC’s forward-looking perspective: the more the execution path can change, the more important orchestration becomes. As AI lets organizations define outcomes, dependencies, and boundaries rather than every workflow step, less predetermined execution requires more deliberate orchestration to keep outcomes reliable and governed.

Who took part in the research?

+

This first edition of BMC’s annual survey is based on 1,982 professionals who actively use orchestration or workload automation, surveyed July 27 to August 21, 2026. Respondents represented nine countries: the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Brazil, France, Germany, Italy, Mexico, and Spain. 62% held management or executive roles, with IT operations managers the largest group (42%). 36% of organizations employ fewer than 1,000 people, 44% employ 1,000 to 9,999, and 13% employ 10,000 or more.
